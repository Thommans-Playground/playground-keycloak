# Make AI walkthroughs force you to think

**The main finding is that an AI walkthrough teaches well only when you do the thinking before the AI shows its answer.** The strongest evidence says to ask you to predict, recall and explain, and to give you feedback straight away. It also says to bring questions back after a day or more, and to check your learning with the AI switched off. The AI-tutor studies agree on one point. AI that hands over answers raises your score during the task and lowers what you can do without it. AI that gives hints, asks questions and explains does much less harm. This matters for you because you feel out of depth, and "that makes sense" feels like learning when it often is not. Almost none of the evidence comes from professional engineers learning a real codebase. It comes mostly from university students, school pupils, crowd workers and lab tasks. Several of the figures below were confirmed only from search summaries, and the notes flag those. So the redesign at the end is a set of well-motivated bets. Nobody has tested it as a package in this setting. Measure it on yourself, as the last section describes.

## Feeling that you understood is not evidence that you will remember

The best-supported finding is that **testing yourself beats rereading or listening to a clear explanation**. A meta-analysis read in full by the researchers found an overall effect of **g = 0.50** for testing against restudy ([Rowland 2014](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf)). (Hedges g is a standard effect size. About 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large.) Recall questions beat recognition questions (free recall 0.81, cued recall 0.72, recognition 0.36). Feedback roughly doubled the benefit (0.73 with feedback, 0.39 without). The benefit was larger when the test came a day or more later (0.69 against 0.41). A second meta-analysis of classrooms, covering 222 studies, found almost the same size (g = 0.499) ([Yang et al. 2021, abstract](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1390918175115857920)); the researchers saw only a summary of it. Transfer to new questions is real but smaller, **d = 0.40**. It is strongest for application and inference questions and weakest for material that was not itself tested ([Pan & Rickard 2018](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PR_2018.pdf)). That paper also notes that after a correction for publication bias, transfer was often near zero when the practice was shallow. So the question has to ask for real elaboration, not a yes or no.

The reason to build this in is that your own feeling is unreliable. In a classic study, repeated studying beat testing on a 5-minute test and raised learners' confidence, yet testing won by a wide margin after a week (about 61% against 40%) ([Roediger & Karpicke 2006](https://psychology.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/216/2019/03/Roediger-Karpicke-2006.pdf); the researchers confirmed it only through summaries). Students in another study predicted concept mapping would beat retrieval, and retrieval was about 50% better a week later ([Karpicke & Blunt 2011](https://www.purdue.edu/newsroom/research/2011/110120KarpickeScience.html)). Learners also rated massed study as better than spaced study after scoring worse with it ([Kornell & Bjork 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18578849/)). The overview paper that rated ten study techniques put practice testing and spaced practice at high utility and rereading and highlighting at low ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/?p=82014)). No peer-reviewed study of retrieval practice on code comprehension or onboarding turned up. The size of the effect for a walkthrough is unknown, and any claim that it works there is an inference.

Spacing is the second strong result. The best gap between repeats grows with how long you need to remember, but as a share of that time it shrinks, so the popular "10-20% rule" is not in the paper ([Cepeda et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/)). Delaying the first retrieval is what helped long-term retention, and expanding versus equal gaps made little lasting difference ([Karpicke & Roediger 2007](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/expanding-retrieval-practice-promotes-short-term-retention-but-eq/)). The researchers' own, unvalidated schedule is to re-ask at the end of the session, the next day, about 3-4 days later, and about 1-2 weeks later. Do not spend effort on a clever expanding algorithm.

## Predict first, explain why, and let support fade

Generating an answer beats reading it, at roughly **d = 0.40** ([Bertsch et al. 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17645161/); subtype figures second-hand). A wrong guess before the reveal can help if feedback follows promptly ([Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19586265/)). That finding is contested. One study found no benefit with a short delay before study ([Grimaldi & Karpicke 2012](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2012/2012_Grimaldi_Karpicke_MC.pdf)). Another found harm for unrelated word pairs ([Knight 2012](https://amalab.uta.edu/files/2021/12/knight-2012-2.pdf)). These were words and facts, not code, so the guess should be plausible and related to what you know. Confidently wrong answers are corrected more often than unsure ones, a finding called hypercorrection ([Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001, via review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3970786)). That is why a confidence rating next to each prediction is useful. The rating exposes the errors worth fixing. The notes give no direct test of this in a tutor, so it is the researchers' suggestion.

Prompted self-explanation helped on average across 64 studies, but nearly all used undergraduates ([Bisra et al. 2018](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/self-explanation-powerful-learning-technique-according-meta-analysis-64-studies)). In the original talk-aloud work, weaker learners explained little, re-read examples looking for something to copy, and monitored their own gaps poorly ([Chi et al. 1989](https://serc.carleton.edu/resources/19512.html)). In a chat that looks like "makes sense" with nothing behind it. "Why" questions about stated facts, called elaborative interrogation, earn only a moderate rating with limited classroom evidence ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://stafforini.com/works/dunlosky-2013-improving-students-learning/)). They need some prior knowledge, so give a worked reason first and then ask a variation. Teach-back (explaining to an imagined newcomer) shows small to moderate effects: **g = 0.35** for preparing to teach and **0.56** for teaching ([Kobayashi 2019, via search digest](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02755)). A later meta-analysis found a smaller overall effect of g = 0.27 ([Kobayashi 2024](https://x-mol.com/paper/1763030854519197696)). Interactive teaching helped more. The evidence that you work harder for a pupil than for yourself comes from fifth graders with a software agent ([Chase et al. 2009](https://purl.stanford.edu/zm369yx3532)), so it does not support the claim for adults.

For a true beginner, a worked example comes before problem-solving. Studying worked examples beat solving the equivalent problems in the original algebra work ([Sweller & Cooper 1985](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worked-example_effect); the notes call this a weak source). Removing the last steps of a solved example, one at a time, eases the move to doing it alone ([Renkl et al. 2002](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11251-009-9107-8); the researchers found no effect sizes). The counterweight is expertise reversal. Guidance that helps novices becomes neutral or harmful as skill grows ([Kalyuga et al. 2003](https://ro.uow.edu.au/edupapers/136)). A 2025 meta-analysis of 176 effect sizes found the effect robust, and found that helping novices mattered more than withholding help from experts ([Tetzlaff et al. 2025, summary](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/one-size-doesnt-fit-all-teaching-experts-and-novices/)). Hence the walkthrough should fade. Narrate the first use case fully, drop the last step of the second and ask you to supply it, and by the third let you lead.

Interleaving is a later-stage tool. Across 59 studies it averaged **g = 0.42** but varied widely (I-squared 77%). It helped most with similar categories and complex material and hurt for word material ([Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/)). A cognitive-load argument says "desirable difficulties" turn harmful when the material has many interacting parts and you lack expertise ([Chen et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01483/pdf)); that comes from one research camp. No evidence exists for interleaving in code reading or architecture learning. The sensible default is to block the first pass and mix examples once two flows are known, for example "here is a snippet, which layer is it, and why?". Splitting material into steps with a check after each helped retention and transfer modestly and cost learning time ([Rey et al. 2019](https://is.mpg.de/publications/rey2018segmenting)). No source gives the right length of a step, so one hop of the sequence diagram per message is the researchers' inference.

## What code-reading research adds, and how thin it is

Program-comprehension theory fits a walkthrough of an unfamiliar system. Experts form a hypothesis and test it top-down, and switch opportunistically to bottom-up reading when the domain is new ([Letovsky, via course reading](https://www.st.cs.uni-saarland.de/edu/empirical-se/2006/PDFs/brien03.pdf); [von Mayrhauser & Vans](https://www.cs.colostate.edu/~somlo/pubs/ijhcs99.pdf), studied on professionals). Names and signatures act as beacons that hint at purpose ([Siegmund et al. 2017](https://www.se.cs.uni-saarland.de/publications/docs/SPP+17.pdf)), though that was an 11-person brain-scan study and the notes say not to build on it. Novices tend to narrate line by line rather than by purpose, and rewording the question did not fix that ([Whalley et al. 2006](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/12626/1/2008001537.pdf)). Many novices also predicted code output badly ([Lister et al. 2004](https://www.cs.auckland.ac.nz/~j-hamer/ITiCSE04-wg.pdf)). Both are intro-course populations. So ask for "what is this step for?" and not only "what does this line do?".

PRIMM (Predict, Run, Investigate, Modify, Make) is the best-known reading-first method. Evidence is encouraging but mostly teacher-reported, in 493 school pupils aged 11-14, with no attainment effect sizes found ([Sentance et al.](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/epub/10.1080/08993408.2019.1608781)). Parsons problems (put shuffled steps in order) saved time but showed no significant learning gain in one study, and students can solve them by surface cues ([NSF listing](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10510497)). Use ordering tasks as a warm-up, then add a "why".

For tests and diagrams the evidence is thin. Fluent assertions cut time to understand assertions in a 51-student experiment ([Leotta et al.](https://sepl.dibris.unige.it/publications/2018-leotta-QUATIC.pdf)), and developers use documentation to build a first mental model of code under test ([Aniche et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.01783)). Reading the test first and predicting its assertions from its title is therefore a reasonable inference. "Break a check and predict which test fails" has no study behind it. UML class and sequence diagrams gave an average of about 12% better comprehension across aggregated experiments, mostly with students ([Scanniello, Gravino et al. 2018](https://www.iris.unisa.it/retrieve/e2915b35-290e-8981-e053-6605fe0a83a3/EMSE_2018_gravino_risi.pdf)). The benefit depended on experience with the notation ([Torchiano et al. 2017](https://iris.polito.it/handle/11583/2645071)). On onboarding, the barriers newcomers report are finding a way to start, documentation, code complexity and social contact ([Steinmacher et al. 2014](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-3-642-55128-4_21.pdf)). One industry study of a 30K-line legacy codebase found expert-guided tours scored 83% on quizzes against 57% for AI-only tours ([Lacy, arXiv 2603.25391](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25391)). That is the authors' own study and a preprint, so treat it as suggestive.

## What goes wrong when an AI is the tutor

The pattern across studies is consistent: **unrestricted AI helps the task and hurts the learning.** In a field experiment with about 1,000 high-school maths students, a plain ChatGPT-like tool raised practice performance 48% but cut exam scores 17% once access was removed. A hint-based tutor raised practice 127% and left exam scores about equal to the control ([Bastani et al., PNAS 2025, via Wharton summary](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education/)). The researchers could not open the paper itself, so the numbers are second-hand. Note what the second result means: guardrails removed the harm, but did not produce a net exam gain.

The closest study to your situation is a preprint from Anthropic, so it is vendor research. Paid crowd workers learned an unfamiliar Python library for about 35 minutes. The AI group scored about **17% lower** on a quiz taken without AI (d = 0.738, p = 0.010), with no significant speed gain. The biggest gap was in debugging ([Shen & Tamkin 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245v2)). Within the AI group, people who delegated, leaned on the AI more over time, or debugged by repeatedly asking it scored 24-39%. People who asked conceptual questions, or generated code and then asked comprehension questions, scored 65-86%. The groups had 2 to 7 people each and were not statistically tested. The study used one library and one hour, a chat interface only, and no comparison with human help. A second preprint reached a similar split: using the AI in place of your own work raised topic volume and lowered understanding, while asking for explanations raised understanding ([Lehmann et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047); the researchers did not read the effect sizes).

Overconfidence is the quiet part. Struggling novices with Copilot finished tasks while keeping an "illusion of competence" and not noticing they had fallen behind ([Prather et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.17739v1)), in a study of 21 students. University students using ChatGPT improved essays but not knowledge or transfer, and showed fewer metacognitive processes such as evaluating their own work ([Fan et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.09315), 117 students). A survey of 319 knowledge workers linked higher confidence in the AI to less critical thinking, and some said they could not verify AI output without domain knowledge ([Lee et al., CHI 2025](https://advait.org/files/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf)); that is self-report, not a learning outcome. The widely shared brain-scan essay study is a preprint with 18 people in its crossover session and is about essays, so it is weak evidence for code ([Kosmyna et al.](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/your-brain-on-chatgpt/)).

Two more pitfalls bear directly on the design. First, accuracy: in more than 2,500 student questions, explanations were conceptually accurate about 90% of the time for compile-time errors and 75% for run-time errors, and the model often ignored an instruction not to give solution code ([CodeHelp follow-up](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.16984)). A beginner cannot reliably spot the errors. A prompt alone cannot guarantee "no answers", so the skill should keep explicit reveal steps and ground every claim in real file lines. The grounding benefit is the researchers' inference, with no direct test. Second, engagement: when Khanmigo withheld answers and asked questions, students largely stopped using it, and effects were small (about 0.06-0.08 SD over a school year) ([Oreopoulos & Low](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai26-1551.pdf); a working paper, seen in summary only). Pure refusal fails too, which argues for a hint ladder with a visible way out. On the positive side, a tightly scoped AI tutor built around the instructor's objectives beat in-class active learning in one 194-student physics trial ([Kestin et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)), though over a short time at one university. A walkthrough tutor on a known codebase looks more like that than like a general chatbot, and that resemblance is an inference. Bloom's famous "2 sigma" tutoring figure is disputed, and most studies land at about 0.4-0.9 SD ([Von Hippel](https://www.educationnext.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/ednext_XIV_2_von_hippel.pdf), seen in summary only). Step-level feedback did as well as human tutoring in one review (d about 0.76 against 0.79) ([VanLehn 2011](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ946764)).

## What is established, what is contested, and what is inference

| Claim | Strength | Main caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Testing beats restudy, more with recall questions, feedback and a delay | Strong (two meta-analyses, g about 0.5) | Students and lab or classroom tasks; no code study |
| Spacing beats massing; delay the first re-test | Strong on direction; schedule is inference | Verbal material; no fixed percentage rule |
| Unrestricted AI hurts learning without AI; hint-based AI removes most of the harm | Moderate (one peer-reviewed field trial, several preprints) | Pupils and crowd workers; numbers second-hand; no professional-engineer study |
| Learners overrate their own understanding with AI | Moderate, consistent across small studies | Small samples; no test of specific fixes in a tutor |
| Generation and prediction before reveal; hypercorrection | Moderate (generation d about 0.40); pretesting contested | Words and facts, not code |
| Self-explanation prompts; teach-back | Moderate, small to medium; most studies on undergraduates | Interactive form helps more; novices need a worked model first |
| Worked examples then fading; reduce help as skill grows | Moderate to strong in theory; expertise reversal is robust | Algebra and physics; extrapolated to architecture |
| Interleaving | Contested and heterogeneous (g about 0.42) | Later stage only; none for code |
| Reading the test first, break-and-predict, Parsons-style ordering, sequence diagrams | Weak to thin | Student samples or no study |
| Obsidian notes, confidence ratings in a tutor, hint ladders in LLM tutors | Researchers' inference | Nothing in the notes tests these |

## Changes to make to the walkthrough skill, ranked

The ranking weighs the strength of evidence and the cost of the change. None has been tested together in a software-onboarding setting, so the combined effect is unknown. The Obsidian parts are design proposals. The notes contain no evidence about note-taking tools, so I do not claim any.

1. **Predict, then reveal, with a confidence rating (evidence: moderate, mixed).** Before each code block, ask what you expect it to do and how sure you are (guess, fairly sure, certain). Then show the real code and name any confident miss. Keep guesses plausible and tied to what you already know. This leans on the generation effect and hypercorrection; the confidence rating itself is untested in tutors.
2. **Replace "got it?" with one cued-recall or "why" question per turn (strong for retrieval, moderate for the format).** Keep the code hidden for the question, give feedback straight away, and wait for your answer before the next step. Prefer application and inference questions ("what breaks if this port is removed?") over recognition. The one-question rule is a load-control inference.
3. **Retrieval opener at the next session, and a re-ask list (strong).** Open with 2-3 questions from last time, answered without notes, then reveal. Re-ask the missed ones at about 3-4 days and 1-2 weeks. Log them in Obsidian. The schedule is the researchers' own and not validated.
4. **Learner produces, AI explains and checks (moderate).** Leave the key lines or the next step for you to write, as the Learning-style TODO markers do, and require one explain-back before moving on if the AI showed code. This follows the high-scoring interaction patterns. It is untested in this setting, and the one product posture the notes cite has no outcome data.
5. **Hint ladder with a visible exit (moderate for hints, inference for the ladder).** Nudge, then pointed hint (name the file and line), then a partial step, then the answer after a real attempt. Make "show me" a logged choice. This avoids the answer-dumping harm and the disengagement seen when a tutor only refuses.
6. **Ground every claim in real code (inference, low cost).** Read the file when answering, quote path and line numbers, say "I have not checked" when true, and invite you to run the test or code. Error rates of 10-25% in one study make this worth doing. No study tests the fix itself.
7. **Self-explanation and teach-back in Obsidian (moderate).** After the worked example and a self-explanation round, ask for a three-sentence explanation to an imagined new hire, written in your own words in your own note, then correct it against the source with a naive "newcomer" follow-up. Do this after you have seen a model explanation, since a learner who does not yet know the material can only repeat it. Writing it in your note, and having the AI read it next session, is the Obsidian proposal.
8. **Fade support as you improve (moderate to strong in theory).** Fully narrate use case one, drop the last step in use case two, let you lead in use case three. Probe with a short prediction first to estimate your level, and cut glosses when you answer before the AI finishes.
9. **Block first, then interleave (contested).** One flow per sitting, one layer at a time. After two flows, mix snippets and ask "which layer, and why?". Drop it if you are overloaded.
10. **Open each use case with a one-sentence purpose and a hypothesis, and ask for purpose summaries (weak to moderate).** Ask "which layers do you expect this touches?" first, then "what is this step for?" per step. Point at names and signatures as beacons, and spend one minute on how to read the sequence diagram.
11. **Test-first reading and break-and-predict (weak).** Read the test, guess its assertions from its title, and predict which test fails if you remove a check. Treat it as a cheap exercise, not a proven one.
12. **Parsons-style ordering of the request path (weak).** A warm-up that saves time, paired with a "why" question.
13. **Productive failure, sparingly (contested).** Ask "how would you stop a deactivated user using an old token?" before showing the design, only on concepts where you have some prior knowledge. Do not use it for syntax or for topics that are entirely new.

Also add a pre-written glossary line the first time a term appears (OIDC, JWT), and warn where an analogy breaks. The notes found no verified literature on analogies, so this is inference only.

## How to tell whether you are really learning

Do not trust the signals the session itself produces. In the maths trial and the Python study, task completion and practice scores did not predict later learning, and students overestimated what they had learned ([Bastani et al.](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education/); [Shen & Tamkin](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245v2)). The notes found no validated instrument for a code-along, so the following is a proposal modelled on those two studies.

First, run a **delayed check with no AI** one to two weeks later: a short quiz that mixes conceptual questions, code reading and debugging, as in the Python study. Second, use **transfer tasks** that change a surface feature while keeping the architecture: a different endpoint, role or flow, asking you to predict behaviour or say which files change. Third, do **teach-back with nothing on screen**, rated against a short rubric you write after the walkthrough. Fourth, track **calibration**: compare your confidence ratings with your actual answers across sessions and watch whether the gap shrinks. Fifth, keep **process logs** of unaided attempts before each hint, "show me" uses and the highest hint level reached, because delegation was the pattern linked to poor outcomes. The notes also suggest comparing sessions with and without the tutor on the delayed test if you have a team.

## Conclusion

The research does not say "add a quiz". It says the order matters: attempt, then feedback, then wait, then retrieve again. Everything else is a lever on that loop. The most useful correction for a new engineer is that comfort is the warning sign. A smooth walkthrough that leaves you feeling clear is the condition under which people overrate themselves, and the hardest-feeling parts (predicting, recalling next week, explaining without notes) are the parts with evidence behind them. The caveat is as important as the advice. The data come from pupils, undergraduates and short lab sessions, and no study tested these techniques inside a code tutor with working engineers. Treat the ranked list as a hypothesis, instrument it with the delayed AI-free check, and drop whatever does not show up there.
